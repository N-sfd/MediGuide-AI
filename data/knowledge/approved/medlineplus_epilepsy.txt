Epilepsy: Patient Education Overview

What epilepsy is

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder characterized by a tendency to
have recurring, unprovoked seizures, which happen when there is a
sudden burst of abnormal electrical activity in the brain. A single
seizure does not necessarily mean a person has epilepsy; epilepsy is
generally diagnosed after a person has had two or more unprovoked
seizures.

Common symptoms

Seizure symptoms vary depending on the type and the part of the
brain involved. They can include staring spells, temporary confusion,
uncontrollable jerking movements of the arms and legs, loss of
awareness, or stiffening of the body. Some seizures are brief and
subtle, while others are more prolonged and involve the whole body.

Common causes

Epilepsy can result from a variety of causes, including genetic
factors, brain injury, stroke, infections affecting the brain, brain
tumors, or developmental disorders. In many cases, a specific cause
is never identified.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis typically involves a detailed medical history, a
description of the seizure events, a neurological examination, and
tests such as an electroencephalogram (EEG) and brain imaging to
help identify the type of seizures and any underlying cause.

General management

Epilepsy is often managed with anti-seizure medications, which help
many people become seizure-free or significantly reduce seizure
frequency. Other approaches, such as dietary therapy, devices, or
surgery, may be considered for some people, particularly if
medications alone do not control seizures. A neurologist can help
create a personalized treatment plan.

When to seek care

Call emergency services for a seizure lasting longer than five
minutes, repeated seizures without full recovery in between,
difficulty breathing after a seizure, or if the person is injured,
pregnant, or has diabetes. Anyone experiencing a new or unexplained
seizure should be evaluated by a qualified healthcare professional.
