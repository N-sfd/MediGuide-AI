Rheumatoid Arthritis: Patient Education Overview

What rheumatoid arthritis is

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune and inflammatory
condition in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's
own joint tissue, causing inflammation. Over time, this can lead to
joint damage, deformity, and disability if not managed. RA can also
affect other body systems, including the skin, eyes, lungs, and
blood vessels.

Common symptoms

Common symptoms include joint pain, swelling, warmth, and stiffness,
often affecting the same joints on both sides of the body, such as
the hands, wrists, and knees. Morning stiffness lasting longer than
30 minutes to an hour is common. Other symptoms can include fatigue,
low-grade fever, and a general feeling of being unwell. Symptoms
often flare and improve over time.

Risk factors

Risk factors include being female, having a family history of RA,
smoking, and certain genetic factors. RA most commonly develops in
middle adulthood, though it can occur at any age.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis typically involves a physical examination, blood tests
(such as rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies), and imaging
tests such as X-rays or ultrasound to assess joint damage and
inflammation.

General treatment approaches

RA is generally managed with medications that reduce inflammation
and slow joint damage, including disease-modifying antirheumatic
drugs (DMARDs) and biologic therapies, often alongside pain
management and physical or occupational therapy. Early, ongoing
treatment from a rheumatologist can help reduce joint damage and
maintain function.

When to seek care

Anyone experiencing persistent joint pain, swelling, or stiffness,
especially affecting multiple joints or lasting several weeks,
should be evaluated by a qualified healthcare professional, since
early diagnosis and treatment can help prevent long-term joint
damage.
