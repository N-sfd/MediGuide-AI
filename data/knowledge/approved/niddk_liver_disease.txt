Liver Disease: Patient Education Overview

What the liver does

The liver is a large organ located in the upper right abdomen that
performs many essential functions, including filtering toxins from
the blood, producing bile to help digest fat, storing energy,
processing nutrients, and making proteins needed for blood clotting.
"Liver disease" is a general term for many different conditions that
can damage the liver or affect how well it works.

Common types of liver disease

- Fatty liver disease: A buildup of fat in the liver, which can be
  associated with obesity, diabetes, or high alcohol use. It can
  sometimes progress to inflammation and scarring if not managed.
- Hepatitis: Inflammation of the liver, most often caused by viral
  infections (such as hepatitis A, B, or C), but also by alcohol use,
  certain medications, or autoimmune conditions.
- Cirrhosis: Long-term scarring of the liver, usually resulting from
  years of ongoing liver damage from causes such as chronic hepatitis
  or heavy alcohol use, which can eventually impair liver function.
- Liver cancer: Cancer that begins in the liver, which is more common
  in people with long-standing liver damage, such as cirrhosis or
  chronic hepatitis B or C infection.

Common symptoms

Early liver disease often causes no symptoms. As it progresses,
symptoms can include fatigue, loss of appetite, nausea, abdominal
pain or swelling, yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice), dark
urine, pale stools, easy bruising or bleeding, and itchy skin.

Risk factors

Risk factors vary by type of liver disease but commonly include
heavy alcohol use, obesity, type 2 diabetes, viral hepatitis
infection, exposure to certain toxins or medications, and a family
history of liver disease.

General prevention and management

Protective habits include limiting alcohol intake, maintaining a
healthy weight, getting vaccinated against hepatitis A and B where
appropriate, avoiding sharing needles or personal items that could
carry blood-borne infections, and using medications only as
directed. Ongoing liver disease is generally managed by treating the
underlying cause and monitoring liver function regularly.

When to seek care

Anyone with symptoms such as yellowing of the skin or eyes,
persistent abdominal pain or swelling, unexplained fatigue, or easy
bruising or bleeding should be evaluated by a qualified healthcare
professional. Regular checkups are especially important for people
with known risk factors for liver disease.
