Parkinson's Disease: Patient Education Overview

What Parkinson's disease is

Parkinson's disease is a progressive disorder of the nervous system
that affects movement. It develops when nerve cells that produce
dopamine, a chemical messenger involved in coordinating movement,
become damaged or die. Symptoms usually develop gradually and worsen
over time.

Common symptoms

Common motor symptoms include tremor, often starting in a hand;
stiffness or rigidity in the limbs or trunk; slowness of movement;
and impaired balance or coordination. Non-motor symptoms can include
changes in sense of smell, sleep problems, constipation, mood
changes, and, in some people, cognitive changes over time. Symptoms
and their progression vary widely from person to person.

Risk factors

The greatest known risk factor is increasing age. Other factors
associated with risk include family history, certain genetic changes,
and, in some studies, exposure to certain environmental toxins.
Parkinson's disease is somewhat more common in men than women.

Diagnosis

There is no single definitive test for Parkinson's disease. Diagnosis
is generally based on medical history, a neurological examination,
and response to certain medications, sometimes supported by imaging
tests to rule out other conditions.

General management

There is currently no cure for Parkinson's disease, but medications,
particularly those that increase or substitute for dopamine, can help
manage symptoms. Physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech
therapy, and regular exercise can also help maintain function. In
some cases, surgical treatments may be considered. A neurologist can
help create a personalized management plan.

When to seek care

Anyone experiencing new tremor, stiffness, slowness of movement, or
balance problems should discuss these symptoms with a qualified
healthcare professional for evaluation.
