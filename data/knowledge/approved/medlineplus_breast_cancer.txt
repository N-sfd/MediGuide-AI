Breast Cancer: Patient Education Overview

What breast cancer is

Breast cancer occurs when cells in the breast grow out of control,
usually forming a tumor. It can begin in different parts of the
breast and, if not treated, can spread to other parts of the body.
Breast cancer can occur in anyone, though it is far more common in
women.

Common signs

Signs can include a new lump or mass in the breast or underarm,
swelling or thickening of part of the breast, skin dimpling or
irritation, breast or nipple pain, nipple retraction, redness or
flaking of the nipple or breast skin, or unusual nipple discharge.
Many breast changes are not cancer, but any new or unusual change
should be evaluated by a healthcare provider.

Risk factors

Risk factors include increasing age, a personal or family history of
breast cancer, certain inherited gene changes such as BRCA1 or
BRCA2, early menstruation or late menopause, dense breast tissue,
and certain reproductive and hormonal factors. Having risk factors
does not mean a person will develop breast cancer, and some people
with breast cancer have no known risk factors.

Screening

Screening mammograms can help find breast cancer early, often before
symptoms appear, when it may be easier to treat. Recommended
screening schedules vary based on age and individual risk factors, so
it is best to discuss an appropriate screening plan with a healthcare
provider.

General treatment overview

Treatment depends on the type and stage of breast cancer and may
include surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, or
targeted therapy, often used in combination. A care team works with
each patient to determine an appropriate treatment plan.

When to seek care

Anyone who notices a new lump, unusual pain, skin changes, or nipple
changes in the breast should contact a healthcare professional for
evaluation. Discuss personal risk factors and an appropriate
screening schedule with a qualified healthcare professional.
