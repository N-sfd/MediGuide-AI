Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS): Patient Education Overview

What IBS is

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a common disorder that affects the
large intestine, causing a group of symptoms that occur together.
Unlike some other digestive conditions, IBS does not damage the
digestive tract or increase the risk of colorectal cancer, but it
can significantly affect quality of life.

Common symptoms

Common symptoms include abdominal pain or cramping, often relieved
by having a bowel movement, along with changes in bowel habits such
as diarrhea, constipation, or alternating between the two. Other
symptoms can include bloating, excess gas, and mucus in the stool.
Symptoms often come and go and can be triggered by certain foods,
stress, or hormonal changes.

Possible contributing factors

The exact cause of IBS is not fully understood, but contributing
factors may include abnormal muscle contractions in the intestine,
increased sensitivity to pain in the gut, changes in gut bacteria,
past gastrointestinal infections, and stress or anxiety, which can
worsen symptoms.

Diagnosis

IBS is typically diagnosed based on a pattern of symptoms and by
ruling out other conditions with similar symptoms, sometimes using
blood tests, stool tests, or other studies as needed.

General management

Management often includes dietary changes (such as identifying and
limiting trigger foods, and sometimes a structured elimination diet
under professional guidance), stress management, regular physical
activity, and medications targeted at specific symptoms, such as
those for pain, diarrhea, or constipation. A healthcare provider or
dietitian can help develop a personalized management plan.

When to seek care

Anyone with persistent digestive symptoms should be evaluated by a
qualified healthcare professional, especially if symptoms include
unexplained weight loss, rectal bleeding, anemia, or symptoms that
begin after age 50, since these can indicate a condition other than
IBS that needs further evaluation.
