Bipolar Disorder: Patient Education Overview

What bipolar disorder is

Bipolar disorder is a mental health condition marked by shifts in
mood, energy, and activity levels that go beyond normal ups and
downs. It involves episodes of mania or hypomania (elevated,
expansive, or irritable mood with increased energy) and episodes of
depression, with periods of more stable mood in between.

Common symptoms

During manic or hypomanic episodes, symptoms can include feeling
unusually energetic or euphoric, decreased need for sleep, racing
thoughts, rapid speech, increased risk-taking behavior, and
distractibility. During depressive episodes, symptoms can include
persistent sadness, loss of interest in activities, fatigue,
difficulty concentrating, changes in sleep or appetite, and, in some
cases, thoughts of death or suicide. The pattern and severity of
episodes vary by the specific type of bipolar disorder and by
individual.

Risk factors

Bipolar disorder often results from a combination of genetic,
biological, and environmental factors. Risk factors include a family
history of bipolar disorder, and it typically first appears in the
late teens or early adulthood, though it can occur at any age.

General treatment approaches

Bipolar disorder is a lifelong condition that is generally managed
with ongoing treatment, most often a combination of medication (such
as mood stabilizers) and psychotherapy. Maintaining consistent sleep
routines, avoiding alcohol and recreational drugs, and tracking mood
patterns can also support treatment. A mental health professional can
help develop and adjust a personalized long-term treatment plan.

When to seek care

Anyone experiencing symptoms of mania, hypomania, or depression that
affect daily life should reach out to a qualified healthcare or
mental health professional for evaluation. If you or someone you know
is having thoughts of suicide or self-harm, seek help immediately:
call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (in the
United States), or call emergency services.
