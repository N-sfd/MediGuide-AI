Diabetes: Patient Education Overview

What diabetes is

Diabetes is a chronic health condition that affects how the body
turns food into energy. Most of the food a person eats is broken
down into sugar (glucose) and released into the bloodstream. The
pancreas normally responds by releasing insulin, a hormone that acts
like a key to let blood sugar into the body's cells for use as
energy.

With diabetes, the body either does not make enough insulin or
cannot use the insulin it makes as well as it should. When not
enough blood sugar moves into cells, it stays in the bloodstream,
and over time this can lead to serious health problems.

Main types of diabetes

- Type 1 diabetes: The body does not make insulin, because the
  immune system attacks the insulin-producing cells in the pancreas.
  It is usually diagnosed in children, teens, and young adults, and
  people with type 1 diabetes need to take insulin every day.
- Type 2 diabetes: The body does not use insulin well and cannot
  keep blood sugar at normal levels. This is the most common type of
  diabetes and develops over many years. It is most often diagnosed
  in adults, though it increasingly affects children and teens too.
- Gestational diabetes: This type develops in pregnant women who
  have never had diabetes before. It usually goes away after the
  baby is born, but it raises the risk of developing type 2 diabetes
  later in life.
- Prediabetes: Blood sugar levels are higher than normal but not yet
  high enough to be diagnosed as type 2 diabetes. Without lifestyle
  changes, prediabetes can progress to type 2 diabetes.

Common symptoms

Common symptoms can include increased thirst, frequent urination,
increased hunger, feeling very tired, blurred vision, numbness or
tingling in the hands or feet, sores that heal slowly, and unplanned
weight loss. Type 1 diabetes symptoms often develop quickly, over
weeks. Type 2 diabetes symptoms often develop slowly, over years,
and some people have no symptoms at all.

Why diabetes matters

Over time, high blood sugar can damage blood vessels and nerves
throughout the body. This can increase the risk of heart disease,
stroke, kidney disease, eye problems, and nerve damage, particularly
in the feet. Managing blood sugar levels helps reduce these risks.

Risk factors for type 2 diabetes

Factors that can increase the risk of type 2 diabetes include being
overweight, being physically inactive, having a family history of
diabetes, older age, having had gestational diabetes, and having
prediabetes.

General lifestyle habits associated with blood sugar management

- Eating balanced meals with attention to carbohydrate portions, as
  advised by a healthcare provider or dietitian.
- Getting regular physical activity as advised by a healthcare
  provider.
- Maintaining a weight that is healthy for the individual.
- Monitoring blood sugar levels as recommended by a healthcare
  provider.
- Taking medications, including insulin, exactly as prescribed.
- Attending regular checkups, including screenings for eye, foot,
  and kidney health.

When to seek care

Anyone experiencing signs of very high or very low blood sugar, such
as confusion, difficulty breathing, rapid heartbeat, loss of
consciousness, or a fruity-smelling breath, should seek emergency
medical care immediately. Anyone with concerns about their risk for
diabetes, or a new diagnosis, should discuss it with a qualified
healthcare professional, who can recommend appropriate testing,
monitoring, and a personalized management plan.
