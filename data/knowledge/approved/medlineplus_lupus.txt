Lupus: Patient Education Overview

What lupus is

Lupus (systemic lupus erythematosus, or SLE, is the most common
type) is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system
attacks the body's own healthy tissue, causing inflammation that can
affect the skin, joints, kidneys, blood cells, brain, heart, and
lungs. Symptoms and severity vary widely and often come and go in
periods called flares and remission.

Common symptoms

Common symptoms include fatigue, joint pain and swelling, a
butterfly-shaped rash across the cheeks and nose, skin rashes that
worsen with sun exposure, fever, chest pain with deep breathing, hair
loss, mouth sores, and sensitivity to sunlight. Because symptoms can
affect many body systems and vary over time, lupus can be challenging
to diagnose.

Risk factors

Lupus is more common in women, particularly during childbearing
years, and disproportionately affects certain racial and ethnic
groups. Risk factors include a family history of lupus or other
autoimmune diseases, certain genetic factors, and environmental
triggers such as sunlight, infections, or certain medications.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis typically involves a review of symptoms and medical
history, a physical examination, blood tests (such as antinuclear
antibody testing), and sometimes urine tests or a biopsy, since no
single test can confirm lupus on its own.

General treatment approaches

There is no cure for lupus, but treatment can help control symptoms
and prevent flares. Treatment often includes anti-inflammatory
medication, antimalarial drugs, corticosteroids, and, for more severe
disease, medications that suppress or modulate the immune system.
Protecting skin from sun exposure and managing stress can also help.
A rheumatologist often coordinates long-term care.

When to seek care

Anyone with persistent joint pain, unexplained rashes, unusual
fatigue, or other symptoms affecting multiple body systems should be
evaluated by a qualified healthcare professional. People with a
lupus diagnosis experiencing a significant flare or new severe
symptoms should contact their care team promptly.
